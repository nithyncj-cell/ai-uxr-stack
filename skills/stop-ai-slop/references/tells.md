# Phrase and structure tells

The categories in this file follow the catalog in hardikpandya/stop-slop (© 2025 Hardik Pandya, MIT License; see `THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md` in the skill folder). The wording, examples and fixes here are written for research writing: reports, insights, plans, emails and slides.

Each entry: what it looks like, why it reads as machine-written, and the fix. Apply fixes to connective prose only. Quotes, numbers, verbatims and citations stay frozen.

---

## 1. Throat-clearing openers

Phrases that announce a point before making it.

- "Here's the thing:", "Here's what we found", "Here's why this matters"
- "The truth is", "The reality is", "The uncomfortable truth is"
- "It turns out that", "What we learned is that"
- "Let me be clear", "I'll be honest"
- "At its core", "In today's [anything]"

**Why it's a tell:** it delays the point and fakes momentum.
**Fix:** delete the opener and start with the subject.

> Before: "Here's the thing: users don't read the fee breakdown."
> After: "Users don't read the fee breakdown."

## 2. Emphasis crutches

Lines that tell the reader something is important instead of showing it.

- "Let that sink in.", "Full stop.", "Period."
- "This matters because", "Make no mistake", "Read that again."

**Fix:** delete. If the point needs weight, add the consequence: who loses what.

## 3. Business jargon

| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| navigate (a challenge) | handle, deal with |
| unpack | explain |
| lean into | accept, commit to |
| landscape (as context) | market, situation |
| deep dive | analysis, close look |
| double down | do more of |
| move the needle | change the number |
| circle back | come back to |
| learnings | lessons, what we found |
| leverage (verb) | use |
| unlock | allow, open up |
| best-in-class, world-class | say what it beats |

Research-specific additions: "pain points" (say the problem), "friction" (say what slows people down), "siloed" (say who doesn't talk to whom), "delight" (say what people liked).

## 4. Adverbs, softeners and hedges

Cut: really, just, literally, genuinely, honestly, simply, actually, deeply, truly, fundamentally, incredibly, extremely, quite, rather, somewhat, arguably.

Keep an adverb only when it changes the meaning ("rarely", "never", "twice"). "Users rarely scrolled" stays. "Users really struggled" becomes "Users struggled", or better, says how.

Hedges in findings ("it seems that", "it could be argued", "may potentially") belong in the base line or the limitations section, not in the headline.

## 5. Meta-commentary

Sentences that narrate the document instead of saying something.

- "In this section, we will explore..."
- "As we'll see below...", "As mentioned earlier..."
- "Let me walk you through..."
- "This report aims to..."

**Fix:** delete, or replace with the first actual finding.

## 6. Binary contrasts

The most common tell. A negated foil set up only to be knocked down.

- "It's not X, it's Y."
- "The problem isn't X. It's Y."
- "Not because X, but because Y."
- "This isn't a feature, it's a mindset."
- "not just X, but also Y"
- "stops being X and starts being Y"

**Fix:** state Y. Keep the contrast only if the data shows both halves and a reader would otherwise assume X.

> Before: "The drop-off isn't about price. It's about trust."
> After: "Trust drives the drop-off. 9 of 12 participants who left named the refund terms; 2 named price."

## 7. Negative listing

Listing what something is not before saying what it is.

- "Not a dashboard. Not a report. A decision tool."
- "It wasn't the price. It wasn't the design. It was the timing."

**Fix:** say what it is, once.

## 8. Dramatic fragmentation

One-word or verbless sentences used for drama.

- "Speed. Clarity. Trust."
- "One question. That's it."
- "The result? Chaos."

**Fix:** write the full sentence. Fragments can survive in a slide title only when the slide body carries the sentence.

## 9. Rhetorical setups

Questions or set-ups the writer answers immediately.

- "So what does this mean? It means..."
- "Why does this matter? Because..."
- "What if I told you..."
- "Think about it."

**Fix:** delete the question and keep the answer.

## 10. False agency

Abstract nouns doing things only people can do.

- "The data tells us...", "The research reveals..."
- "The complaint becomes a fix."
- "The insight drove the redesign."

**Fix:** name the person or team. "We read the data and concluded...", "The design team changed the form after the readout."

## 11. Passive voice that hides the actor

- "Mistakes were made.", "A decision was taken to...", "It was found that..."

**Fix:** front the actor. If the actor is unknown, say so, or use "the team".

## 12. Wh- starters

Sentences that open with What / Why / How / When as a reveal.

- "What we found was surprising."
- "Why this matters is simple."

**Fix:** lead with the subject and verb. "Participants skipped the tutorial."

## 13. Rhythm and word patterns

- **Metronome sentences:** three or more sentences of the same length in a row. Vary them.
- **Punchline endings:** every paragraph ending on a short zinger. Let some end on a plain fact.
- **Reflex triplets:** "fast, simple, and reliable" on every line. Use one or two items when that's what the evidence supports.
- **Staccato over-correction:** after cutting, don't leave a string of five-word sentences. That is its own tell.
- **Signpost pairs:** "Firstly... Secondly... Finally..." in a three-sentence paragraph. Cut the signposts.
- **Permission-granting closers:** "And that's okay.", "That's not a bad thing." Delete.
